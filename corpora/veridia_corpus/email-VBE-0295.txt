Dear Sir/Madam,

I am an MPhil student in Health Data Science at the University of Cambridge,
working on a dissertation about false positive rates in automated haematology
flagging and their downstream effect on laboratory workload.

My supervisor is Dr Helen Ashby-Croft in the Department of Public Health and
Primary Care, and she suggested I contact manufacturers directly rather than
relying only on published literature, since much of the relevant detail is not
published.

I would be very grateful for any of the following, and I completely understand
if some or all of it is commercially confidential:

1. A description of the flagging algorithm categories used on the VeriScan 400
   and 400 Pro - not the algorithm itself, but the taxonomy of flags and what
   each is intended to detect.

2. Any published or shareable data on flag rates in routine use. My literature
   review has manufacturer-quoted sensitivity and specificity figures for
   several platforms but almost nothing on what rate laboratories actually
   experience.

3. The distinction between the validated flag set and the advisory flags added
   in firmware 4.3.0. I found this mentioned in a bulletin available on a
   customer portal that appears to have been indexed publicly, and I am
   interested in how a manufacturer decides which side of that line a flag
   falls on. If that document was not meant to be public please tell me and I
   will not cite it.

4. Whether Veridia would be open to a short interview - 30 to 45 minutes - with
   someone in applications or R&D about how flag thresholds are set and how the
   trade-off between sensitivity and review workload is decided.

I should be honest that I have nothing to offer in return except an
acknowledgement and a copy of the dissertation. I am also aware that a student
asking a company to explain how its algorithms work is a request that could
reasonably be declined.

If it helps: my interest here is not adversarial. My reading of the literature
is that flag rates are the single biggest driver of biomedical scientist
workload in routine haematology and that almost nobody studies them, and I
would like that to be less true.

Thank you for your time.
