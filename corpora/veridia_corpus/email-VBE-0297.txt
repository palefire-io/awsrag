Dear Ms Sundaram,

Thank you for a well-judged email. Taking your points in order.

1. FLAG TAXONOMY. Yes, happily. This is in our Instructions for Use, which is
   not confidential, and I will send you the relevant section. The validated
   flag set covers morphological suspicion (blast, immature granulocyte,
   atypical lymphocyte), distributional abnormality (scatter pattern outside
   expected envelope), analytical interference (platelet clump, nucleated red
   cells, lipaemia, cold agglutinin) and instrument condition (background,
   carryover). Each has a defined trigger condition, though the numerical
   thresholds are confidential.

2. REAL-WORLD FLAG RATES. This is the interesting part of your question and
   the honest answer is that we hold this data and have never published it.

   VeriConnect telemetry gives us flag rates across several hundred
   instruments. The distribution is wider than I would have guessed before I
   looked: the interquartile range across sites for overall differential flag
   rate is roughly 3.5 to 9 per cent, with a long tail above that which is
   almost entirely explained by case mix (haematology-oncology centres flag
   far more) and by sites that have not tuned their thresholds.

   I would need to ask about sharing aggregated data with you. It would need
   to be properly anonymised and I would want Elena's agreement, which is why
   she is copied. I think there is a reasonable chance the answer is yes, in
   which case I would want it done as a proper collaboration with an agreed
   scope rather than as an informal data dump.

3. THE ADVISORY FLAGS. The bulletin you found (VBB-0061) is a customer
   bulletin. It is not secret and if it is publicly indexed that is our
   configuration rather than your trespass. Cite it if it is useful.

   Your question about how we decide which side of the line a flag falls on is
   the best question in your email and I will answer it properly.

   The short version: a flag enters the validated set when we can demonstrate
   its performance against a reference method on a specimen population that
   covers the intended use, and when we have committed to that performance in
   the technical documentation. Advisory flags are ones where the underlying
   signal is real and useful but where either the reference standard is
   contested (what exactly is a "fragment", and would two haematologists
   agree), or the specimen population needed to characterise it is hard to
   assemble because the condition is rare.

   BLAST? is the interesting case. It is a genuinely useful signal and it is
   advisory rather than validated because the consequence of a validated blast
   flag is that laboratories will, correctly, build workflow around it, and the
   evidence we would need to support that is a much higher bar than the
   evidence needed to show the signal exists.

A suggestion, offered as someone who has watched a lot of people study this
badly. The most useful thing you could do for the field is not to characterise
flag rates - people do that - but to characterise what laboratories DO with a
flag. In our data, the same flag at two sites produces a film at one and a
comment at the other, and the difference is local policy, staffing and the time
of day. The workload consequence you are interested in lives in that decision,
not in the flag.

a manufacturer can publish an independent analysis of our own false positive
rates and have it believed. You can.
