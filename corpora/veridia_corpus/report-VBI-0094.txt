1. SUMMARY
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Between 07:12 and 11:06 UTC on Tuesday 11 March 2025, users at 17 VeriConnect
tenants configured for SAML single sign-on were unable to authenticate.
Approximately 1,340 named users were affected. The failure was fail-closed:
VeriConnect refused assertions it could not verify.

The trigger was a scheduled rotation of the SAML signing certificate at the
identity provider. VeriConnect held identity provider metadata cached from
tenant onboarding and never refreshed it, so it continued to trust only the
previous signing certificate.

Analyser operation, HL7 result transmission to laboratory LIS systems, and
data integrity were unaffected. This was an availability incident, not a
security breach. No unauthorised access occurred and no data was lost or
exposed.

3. ROOT CAUSE
-----------------------------------------------------------------
3.1  Immediate cause: VeriConnect could not verify assertion signatures
     because it held a stale copy of the identity provider's signing
     certificate.

3.2  Underlying cause: the SAML integration was implemented with metadata
     fetched once at tenant onboarding and persisted indefinitely. There was
     no refresh mechanism, no staleness monitoring, and no support for
     multiple valid signing certificates during a rotation window.

3.3  This is not an exotic omission. SAML metadata refresh is a standard part
     of a mature implementation and its absence should have been caught in
     design review. The integration was built in 2022 for a single pilot
     customer whose IdP certificate had a five-year validity, and the question
     of rotation was deferred and never revisited when the feature was
     generalised.

3.4  Contributing cause: no alerting on authentication failure rate. The
     first Veridia knew of the outage was a customer ticket 14 minutes after
     it began. With alerting, the team would have been engaged before the
     first customer noticed.

3.5  Contributing cause: only 4 of the 17 affected tenants had break-glass
     local administrator accounts. Those four could have restored
     administrative access themselves within minutes; in fact only one did,
     because the credentials for the other three were not readily accessible
     out of hours. Even where the control existed it had not been tested.

3.6  Contributing cause: manual per-tenant remediation was slow because there
     was no bulk operation. Restoring 17 tenants took 116 minutes of the
     235-minute incident. A bulk refresh tool would have compressed this to
     minutes.

-----------------------------------------------------------------
4. WHAT WENT WELL
-----------------------------------------------------------------
4.1  Fail-closed behaviour was correct. VeriConnect refused assertions it
     could not verify rather than accepting them. An availability incident is
     the right outcome here; the alternative would have been far worse.

4.2  Diagnosis took 88 minutes from the first ticket to root cause. Given
     that the symptom pointed at Veridia's own service and the cause was
     external, this is reasonable.

4.3  The clinical result path was unaffected. Instruments continued to
     analyse samples and to transmit results to laboratory LIS systems
     throughout. This is a direct consequence of the architectural decision
     to keep the instrument-to-LIS path independent of VeriConnect. Sites on
     integration topology A (see spec-vericonnect-integration-v6.txt) had no
     clinical impact at all.

4.4  Support handled 9 tickets and a substantially larger volume of phone
     traffic without losing track of any of it.

4.5  The decision not to deploy a code change under incident conditions was
     correct and was made deliberately rather than by default.

-----------------------------------------------------------------
5. WHAT WENT BADLY
-----------------------------------------------------------------
5.1  Veridia learned of the outage from a customer.

5.2  Customers learned of the outage from their own users. The first Veridia
     communication went out at 08:47, 95 minutes after the first failure and
     81 minutes after the first ticket. That is too late.

5.3  The status page was not updated until 08:47 either. Several customers
     reported checking it early in the incident and being reassured by an
     all-green status that was wrong.

5.4  Break-glass accounts existed in name at four tenants and worked at one.
     A control that is not tested is not a control.

5.5  Laboratories lost access to the QC review and authorisation workflow for
     nearly four hours during a Tuesday morning. Several reverted to
     instrument-local review and paper QC records. Two sites reported a
     backlog that took the rest of the day to clear. The absence of clinical
     harm should not obscure the operational cost.

Two customers (ACC-1103 and ACC-1270) requested formal incident documentation
for their own governance. Both were supplied with VBB-0044 and, under NDA, a
redacted copy of this review.

One customer (ACC-1146) asked whether the audit trail gap during the window
would be a finding at their next ISO 15189 assessment. The answer given: the
gap is an absence of user activity because users could not log in, not an
absence of logging; failed authentications are themselves logged with reason
code VC-4412; and VBB-0044 exists precisely so that the gap can be explained
in an evidence pack. This is now standard guidance.

8. LESSONS
-----------------------------------------------------------------
8.1  A dependency you do not refresh is a dependency you have decided to let
     expire at a time of someone else's choosing.

8.2  Fail-closed is correct and expensive. The cost is borne by the customer,
     which obliges us to make the failure short and to tell them about it
     quickly. We did neither well enough.

8.3  Untested controls are decorative. Break-glass accounts existed at four
     tenants and functioned at one, and the difference was entirely whether
     anyone had ever tried.

8.4  The architectural separation between the clinical result path and the
     cloud platform is the single most valuable design decision in this
     product. It converted what could have been a patient safety incident
     into an operational inconvenience. It should be defended in future
     roadmap discussions, including when it is inconvenient.
